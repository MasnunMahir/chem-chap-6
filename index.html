6.1 Foundations

The mole

A mole is just a counting word — exactly like "dozen" or "hundred" — except built for numbers too large for either to handle.

You already count eggs by the dozen and sheets of paper by the ream. Atoms and molecules need a far bigger counting word, because even a speck of matter holds a staggering number of them.

12
= 1 dozen O₂
100
= 1 hundred O₂
1,000
= 1 thousand O₂
6.023 × 10²³
= 1 mole O₂

That number — 6.02 × 10²³ — is called Avogadro's number, and it works the same way regardless of what you're counting:

1 mole of atoms contains 6.02 × 10²³ atoms

1 mole of molecules contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules

1 mole of ions contains 6.02 × 10²³ ions

So the mole of a substance is simply the amount that contains Avogadro's number of its particles. Conveniently, that amount also has a very practical shortcut: the atomic or molecular mass expressed in grams is exactly 1 mole of that substance. 12 grams of carbon contains 6.02 × 10²³ carbon atoms — so 12 g of C = 1 mole of C.

Finding the molecular mass of a molecule

Add up the atomic masses of every atom in the formula — that total, in grams, is one mole.

Cl₂

2 Cl atoms → 2 × 35.5 = 71
1 mole Cl₂ = 71 g

NaCl

Na + Cl → 23 + 35.5 = 58.5
1 mole NaCl = 58.5 g

CuSO₄·5H₂O

Cu+S+9O+10H → 63.5+32+144+10 = 249.5
1 mole = 249.5 g

6.1.1 Foundations

Molar volume of a gas

For gases, the mole gives you something extra: a fixed volume, regardless of what the gas actually is.

At standard temperature and pressure (0°C and 1 atm — often shortened to STP), 1 mole of any gas occupies exactly 22.4 litres. It doesn't matter whether it's CO₂, H₂, or NH₃ — same mole, same volume.

n = w / M
moles from mass
n = V / 22.4
moles from volume at STP
n = N / 6.023×10²³
moles from particle count

Interactive · gas mole converter

Same converter as before, now with a fourth mode — volume at standard conditions.

grams per mole
Worked example

1 litre of CO₂ at STP: 22.4 L = 6.023×10²³ molecules, so 1 L contains 6.023×10²³ ÷ 22.4 = 2.69 × 10²² molecules.

Worked example

Volume of 10 g H₂ gas at STP: n = 10 ÷ 2 = 5 mol, so V = 5 × 22.4 = 112 litres.

Try it yourself

Set the converter to CH₄, choose "volume", type 5 — that's 5 litres of methane at STP. Each CH₄ molecule has 4 H atoms, so multiply the molecule count by 4 to answer: how many H atoms are in 5 L of CH₄?

6.1.2 Foundations

Mole & molecular formula

A molecular formula is more generous than it looks — one small formula like H₂O quietly encodes seven separate facts.

The gram amount you get from expressing a molecular mass in grams is exactly 1 mole of that substance. Water's molecular formula is H₂O with molecular mass 18 — so 18 grams is "1 gram-molecular-mass of water," or simply 1 mole of water. Unpack that one formula and here's everything it's telling you at once:

H₂O is water.

The formula of 1 molecule of water is H₂O.

The formula of 1 mole of water is H₂O.

1 molecule of H₂O contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.

1 mole of H₂O contains 2 mole hydrogen atoms and 1 mole oxygen atoms.

In 1 mole of H₂O, the H atoms weigh 1×2 = 2 g and the O atom weighs 16×1 = 16 g — total mass = 18 g.

In 1 mole of H₂O: 1.204×10²⁴ H atoms (6.023×10²³×2), 6.023×10²³ O atoms, and 6.023×10²³ H₂O molecules.

6.1.3 Foundations

Molar solution

Dissolve a solute in a solvent and you get a solution. How much you dissolve — measured against the mole — is what molarity means.

Half a glass of water plus a spoon of salt, stirred until the salt disappears: that's a solution, with water as the solvent and salt as the solute.

Solution = Solute + Solvent

"Dilute" and "concentrated" only make sense relative to each other — there's no fixed line between them. More solute in the same solvent (or the same solute in less solvent) always shifts a solution toward "concentrated."

dilute

Drag the slider to add more solute to the same amount of solvent — watch the label shift from dilute toward concentrated. There's no fixed cutoff between the two; it's always a comparison.

The number of moles of solute dissolved in every litre of solution is called the molarity. Dissolve 2 moles in 1 litre and you have a 2‑molar solution. Two special cases get their own names: 0.5 mole/litre is a semimolar solution, and 0.1 mole/litre is a decimolar solution.

Preparing a solution of a given molarity

Select a volumetric flask of the volume you need.

Weigh the exact mass of solute the calculation calls for.

Add some water and shake the flask until it fully dissolves.

Top up with water to the calibration mark on the flask.

One equation ties mass, molarity, volume and molar mass together:

w = (S × V × M) / 1000

w = mass of solute (g)  ·  S = molarity  ·  V = volume (mL)  ·  M = molar mass (g/mol)

6.2 Composition

Percent composition of elements

How many grams of each element hide inside 100 grams of a compound — read straight off its formula.

% composition = (atomic mass × number of atoms × 100) / molecular mass

Take HCl (molecular mass 36.5): 1 g of it holds 1/36.5 g of H, so 100 g holds (1×100)/36.5 = 2.74% H — leaving 97.26% Cl to make up the rest.

6.2.1 Composition

From percent composition to empirical formula

A molecular formula counts every atom. An empirical formula only keeps the simplest ratio between them — H₂O₂ (hydrogen peroxide) reduces all the way down to HO.

STEP 1

Divide each element's percent composition by its atomic mass.

STEP 2

Divide every quotient by the smallest one among them.

STEP 3

If needed, scale all results up to the nearest whole numbers.

STEP 4

Write those whole numbers as subscripts — skip writing a "1".

6.2.2 Composition

From empirical to molecular formula

The empirical formula only gives a ratio. Multiply it by the right whole number and it becomes the true molecular formula.

If the molecular mass is n times heavier than the empirical formula's own mass, then:

Molecular Formula = (Empirical Formula)ₙ   where   n = Molecular Mass ÷ Empirical Formula Mass

Sometimes empirical and molecular formulas are identical — water is H₂O either way, and sulfuric acid is H₂SO₄ either way. But whenever every atom count in a molecular formula shares a common factor, the two formulas split apart:

Glucose

Benzene

C₆H₆ → CH

6.3 Reactions

Chemical reactions & chemical equations

A chemical change swaps a substance's properties for a completely new set. The equation is just the shorthand for that swap.

The starting substances are reactants; whatever forms by the end is a product. A chemical equation writes reactants on the left, products on the right, and connects them with an arrow (→) or an equals sign (=) once balanced.

1 · Sides

Reactants on the left, products on the right, separated by → or =.

2 · Multiple substances

More than one reactant or product? Join them with a plus sign.

3 · Balance

Every element needs the same atom count on both sides — that's "balancing."

4 · Unbalanced is OK sometimes

If balancing isn't the point, an arrow alone is fine without full balancing.

5 · Physical states

(s) solid · (l) liquid · (g) gas · (aq) dissolved in water — written right after each formula.

6 · Heat energy

To show heat produced or absorbed, the equation must be fully balanced with states included.

2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)

C(s) + O₂(g) → CO₂(g)

CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)

When heat is involved

A small delta (Δ) above the arrow means heat was applied. Heating solid magnesium nitrate: 2Mg(NO₃)₂(s) →(Δ) 2MgO(s) + 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g)

6.3.1 Reactions

Balancing chemical equations

Reactants and products are different substances built from the very same atoms — so the count of every element must match on both sides. Multiply formulas by whole numbers until it does.

There's no fixed recipe, but a few habits help: write correct formulas first, balance compound substances before lone elements, and keep adjusting coefficients — never subscripts — until every element checks out.

6.3.2 Reactions

Mole & chemical equation — stoichiometry

A balanced equation is secretly a recipe: it tells you exactly how many moles (and grams) of each reactant combine to give exactly how many moles of product.

The branch of chemistry that turns a balanced equation into these mass/mole predictions is called stoichiometry. Here's the full breakdown for magnesium burning in oxygen:

2Mg(s)+ O₂(g)→ 2MgO(s)
Moles2 mol Mg1 mol O₂2 mol MgO
Particles2×6.023×10²³ atoms6.023×10²³ molecules2×6.023×10²³ molecules
Mass2×24 = 48 g1×32 = 32 g2×40 = 80 g

6.4 Reactions

Limiting reactant

Reactants rarely arrive in exactly the right proportion. Whichever one runs out first cuts the reaction short — everything else is measured against it.

The reactant that's completely used up is called the limiting reactant. It alone decides how much product forms, how much of the other reactant reacts, and how much of that other reactant is left over.

Interactive · 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (by particle count)

Mg atom O₂ molecule faded = left over
Matches the book

4 Mg + 4 O₂ → magnesium runs out first (needs only 2 O₂), leaving 2 O₂ molecules over. Try 70 Mg + 30 O₂ next — now oxygen is the one that runs out.

By mass instead

75 g Cl₂ mixed with 5 g H₂ (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl): 5 g H₂ would need 177.5 g Cl₂ to react completely, but only 75 g Cl₂ is available — so chlorine is the limiting reactant here, and hydrogen is left over.

6.5 Reactions

Percentage of yield

Real reactants are rarely 100% pure, and real purification is never perfect — so real yields almost always fall short of the calculated maximum.

The purest lab-grade substances, called Analar, are still only about 99% pure. Whatever the reason, the actual product collected is compared against the theoretical amount stoichiometry predicts:

% yield = (actual amount obtained ÷ calculated amount) × 100

Hands‑on

Two lab experiments

The theory above is exactly what happens on a bench with a flask, a balance, and a Bunsen burner.

Experiment 1 · Preparing 0.1 molar Na₂CO₃ solution (250 mL)

Principle: sodium carbonate is a primary standard — pure, dry, stable, and easy to weigh directly — which makes it ideal for practising molar solution prep.

250 mL volumetric flask Funnel Weighing bottle Chemical balance Wash bottle Pure Na₂CO₃

Insert a funnel into a clean 250 mL volumetric flask.

Weigh a dry weighing bottle on the chemical balance.

Add sodium carbonate until the bottle is exactly 2.65 g heavier than before (from w = SVM/1000, with S=0.1, V=250, M=106).

Tip the sodium carbonate from the weighing bottle into the flask through the funnel.

Add distilled water slowly; swirl to dissolve fully, then top up exactly to the 250 mL mark.

Caution: use only dry, pure sodium carbonate, keep the weighing bottle dry, and add distilled water only.

Need the calculation for a different molarity or volume? Head back up to the molarity solver and try your own numbers.

Experiment 2 · Percent composition of lattice water in blue vitriol

Principle: blue vitriol (CuSO₄·5H₂O) is copper sulphate locked together with 5 moles of "crystal water." Heat drives that water off as vapour and the crystal turns from blue to white.

CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O

Copper sulphate Desiccator Chemical balance Porcelain bowl Tripod stand + net Bunsen burner

Weigh the empty porcelain bowl — call this mass a. Add copper sulphate and weigh again — call this b, so the sample itself weighs (b − a) g.

Set the bowl on a net over a tripod stand and heat gently and evenly with a Bunsen burner.

Watch the colour fade from blue to white as the crystal water evaporates.

Once the colour change is complete, stop heating.

Cool the bowl quickly in a desiccator (to stop it re‑absorbing moisture) and weigh again — call this c.

Caution: heat slowly and evenly so every spot dries out, and weigh again quickly once heating stops.

The water driven off has mass (b − c) grams, out of a (b − a) gram sample. So:

% crystal water = [(b − c) ÷ (b − a)] × 100%

Zooming out

The big picture

Every calculation in this chapter is really just one idea, worn in different disguises: convert whatever you're given into moles, then convert moles into whatever you actually need.

By mass

  • n = w ÷ M
  • Works for solids, liquids, gases — anything with a molar mass
  • Feeds directly into molarity: w = SVM ÷ 1000

By particle count

  • n = N ÷ 6.023×10²³
  • Ties atoms, molecules and ions to the same Avogadro number
  • The definition the whole chapter is built on

By volume (gases only)

  • n = V ÷ 22.4 at STP
  • Same 22.4 L for every gas — identity doesn't matter
  • Only valid at standard temperature & pressure
"One mole, many masks"

mass, particle count and gas volume are three completely different-looking numbers that always describe the exact same pile of matter — the mole is just the translator between them.
